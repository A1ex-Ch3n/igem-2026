## 1. What has been the most meaningful project, task, or experience you've worked on so far this summer, and why has it stood out to you? (100–150 words)

**A:** The most meaningful part of my summer has been the phage-isolation work — specifically getting our plaque assays to actually work. We collected soil from ten sites at the California Botanic Garden in May, and by early June we had six candidate phages we thought could lyse our *Pseudomonas* isolates. Our first two plating attempts failed or were only partially readable — the soft agar wouldn't fully solidify, so we couldn't tell if we were seeing real lysis or not. Instead of assuming the phages just weren't there, we diagnosed the actual problem (agar concentration, phage volume) and reran the assay with fifteen times more filtrate. Helping prep the media and plates that finally gave us a readable round three, after two rounds that told us nothing, taught me that most of research is patient troubleshooting, not one clean experiment.

## 2. What is one new skill, piece of knowledge, or professional lesson you've gained through your internship or research experience? (100–150 words)

**A:** I learned that a failed result is data, not a dead end. Our first metagenomic DNA extractions came back with almost no detectable DNA in nine of ten soil samples. Rather than just repeating the same protocol harder, our PI reasoned through *why* it likely failed — one gram of soil per column probably saturated the silica column with co-extracted inhibitors that outcompeted DNA for binding — and we redesigned the extraction around a much smaller soil mass per tube, pooling several tubes to recover enough DNA afterward. That taught me to ask "why didn't this work" before "how do I get more sample," and that a well-reasoned protocol change is worth more than brute-force repetition. I've started applying that instinct to my own troubleshooting instead of redoing steps identically and hoping for a different outcome.

## 3. Looking ahead to the second half of the summer, what are you most excited to accomplish, explore, or learn?

**A:** I'm most excited to see our first sequencing data come back — for the three *Pseudomonas* strains we isolated, and eventually for the phages themselves. We already know computationally that our isolates are a close match to a *Pseudomonas* species we can't yet pair with any known phage, so the second half of summer is about closing that gap: screening the phage genomes we've collected, confirming host range experimentally, and testing whether *P. putida* — our best computational "surrogate host" candidate — is actually susceptible to any of our phages. I'm also looking forward to seeing whether the receptor-binding-protein predictions our computational teammates have been building actually hold up against real infection data. That's the moment our wet-lab work and the computational side of the project finally meet.

## 4. Are there any additional comments about your summer experience you would like to share?

**A:** This summer has been my first real experience with the rhythm of experimental research — long stretches of preparation for a single readout, plans that get rewritten mid-week, and results that force you to rethink the next step instead of just executing it. Working alongside my teammates on the wet-lab side, in parallel with the computational half of the project, has shown me how one research question can be attacked from very different angles at once. I've also appreciated how much ownership we've been given over troubleshooting decisions rather than just being handed fixes.

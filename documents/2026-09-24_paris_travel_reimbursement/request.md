## 2. What iGEM Is, and Why Paris

**iGEM is the largest synthetic biology competition in the world.** It was founded
at MIT and is now run by the iGEM Foundation. Each year several hundred university
teams spend a full year designing and building an original biological project, then
present that work at the Grand Jamboree, where it is judged on its science, its
engineering, and the responsibility with which it was carried out. It is one of the
few venues where undergraduate research is held to a professional standard and
evaluated by external judges.

An iGEM project is required to span three disciplines at once:

- **Wet lab** — the physical experimental work, from culturing organisms to
  engineering and testing biological parts.
- **Dry lab** — the computational side: genome analysis, structural prediction, and
  modelling that direct what happens at the bench.
- **Human practices** — the bridge to society, ensuring the project is safe,
  ethical, and addresses a real problem.

Teams are also required to work through the "Design, Build, Test, Learn"
engineering cycle rather than pursuing a single result, and to publish their
methods, parts, and data for other teams to reuse.

**The Grand Jamboree is not an optional conference.** It is the competition's only
judging session. Teams deliver their presentation and poster to judging panels in
person over four days; medals, village awards, and special prizes are all decided
there. A team that completes a year of research but does not attend receives no
evaluation and no award. Attendance is therefore a condition of the work counting at
all, which is why registration and travel are budgeted as part of the project rather
than as a trip.

The competition charges fees in three stages, all payable to the iGEM Foundation in
euros: a team registration fee, a team finalization fee, and a separate Jamboree
ticket for each attending team member. The deadlines for these fall across the year
and well before the November event, which is why the payments in §6 were already
made.

## 4. The 2026 Project

### The problem

Antibiotics kill bacteria indiscriminately, and resistance to them is rising.
Bacteriophages — viruses that infect only bacteria and are harmless to humans — are
one of the few routes to an antibacterial agent that destroys a single target strain
and leaves the rest of a patient's microbial community intact. The obstacle is
specificity itself: each phage kills only a narrow set of strains, and determining
which phage matches which bacterium is still done largely by trial and error.

Our project asks what decides that match, at the level of a single protein.

### What we did

We collected topsoil from ten sites at the California Botanic Garden in Claremont,
isolated *Pseudomonas* bacteria from those samples, and isolated a bacteriophage
capable of killing one of the strains we had recovered. We then sequenced both sides
of that pairing — the bacterial hosts and the phage — and analysed the phage protein
that makes first contact with the bacterial surface.

Milestones 1 and 2 are the ones an outside reader can check in a browser in under a

by undergraduates, and three genome assemblies that anyone in the world can now
download.

### What those milestones mean

**The publication.** *Microbiology Resource Announcements* is a peer-reviewed ASM
journal. Publishing there means the three bacterial genomes we sequenced have been
checked by external reviewers and released as a permanent public resource. The first
author is a CMC undergraduate.

**The phage genome.** Searching those 80 genes against the public databases returns
no match to anything previously described. The most likely reading is that the team
has recovered and sequenced a bacteriophage that no one has characterised before,
found in soil a few miles from campus.

**The safety screen.** Before a phage project may proceed, iGEM requires evidence
that the organism carries no genes that would make it dangerous to work with or to
release. Screening our genome against the standard virulence-factor and
antibiotic-resistance reference databases returned nothing on either. That result is
what permits the rest of the project.

**A change of direction that came from the computational work.** The team had spent
weeks assuming the phage recognises a *protein* on the bacterial surface and had
built a computational screen to find which one. When the three-dimensional structure
of the phage's tail spike was predicted, it was not the compact shape of a
protein-binding protein but a long right-handed helix — the geometry of an enzyme
that cuts sugar chains. The phage does not grip a protein handle; it digests the
bacterium's sugar coat. That result retired the original screen and redefined the
target of the whole project. We also demonstrated, using known-interacting control
pairs, *why* the abandoned screen could not have worked — a negative result worth
reporting to other teams.

**Six engineered protein constructs, now in expression testing.** Guided by that
structure, six protein constructs were designed and ordered from a commercial DNA
synthesis company. They are at the bench now, being tested for expression, so that
the sugar-cutting prediction can be checked experimentally.

### What is still open

The prediction that differences in the bacterial sugar coat explain which strains
this phage can and cannot kill remains a prediction: the assay has been run, but the
readout is not yet in our hands. The protein constructs are in expression testing and
have not yet been confirmed as expressed. A second phage remains to be sequenced.
These are the questions the team carries into the Jamboree.
